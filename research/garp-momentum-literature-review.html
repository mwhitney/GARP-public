Does the GARP + Momentum model hold up?

A literature review of the value, momentum, and quality research behind the scoring formula in scorer.py — where it's well-grounded, where it diverges from how these signals are actually built in the academic and practitioner literature, and what a live check of the current output data found.

The core thesis is sound. Value, momentum, and quality are three of the most replicated anomalies in the empirical asset-pricing literature, and Peter Lynch's own GARP track record (Magellan Fund, ~29%/yr annualized, 1977–90) is real. Combining them into a single blended score — rather than three separate screens — is also exactly what the practitioner integration research recommends.

The current implementation leaves three kinds of gaps, in order of how much they matter: (1) it's missing an entire factor category — profitability/quality — that the literature says matters more than either value or momentum alone when the goal is avoiding value traps and growth-fueled-by-debt; (2) two pieces of the project's original December strategy spec (relative strength vs. a benchmark, interest coverage) were quietly dropped during the Finviz rebuild, and their absence is precisely what the research flags as a weakness; (3) a handful of thresholds (1-month performance, PEG = 1.0) don't match how the academic versions of these signals are actually constructed. None of this invalidates the approach — it's a well-evidenced upgrade list, not a redesign.

1 · Momentum

Real, replicated effect — but the model measures a different shape of it than the research does.

  • The core effect is robust. Jegadeesh & Titman's original test bought the prior 12-month winner decile and sold the loser decile, holding 3–12 months, and found statistically reliable risk-adjusted excess returns; the effect later reverses over multi-year horizons and shows up across nearly every market and asset class tested since. Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993
  • It's a relative signal, not an absolute one. The academic edge comes from ranking stocks against each other (or against a benchmark) — not from checking whether a stock's own return is positive. A stock up 1% while the index is up 15% is a momentum laggard, even though its return is "positive."
  • Trend-following is a related but distinct effect. Time-series momentum — a security's own trailing-12-month return predicting its own future return, independent of any peer ranking — is separately documented and supports SMA-style trend filters specifically. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, 2012
  • Nearness to the 52-week high may be the cleaner anchor. George & Hwang found that proximity to the 52-week high explains a large share of momentum's profitability — arguably more than raw trailing return — and, unlike raw-return momentum, forecasts built on it don't reverse in the long run. George & Hwang, 2004
  • Much of "stock" momentum is actually sector momentum. Once industry returns are stripped out, the residual stock-specific momentum shrinks substantially and at some horizons stops being statistically significant. Moskowitz & Grinblatt, 1999
  • The most recent month is dangerous territory. Short-term reversal — last month's biggest winners underperforming next month — sits right next to momentum in the data, which is why academic momentum construction almost universally skips the most recent month before ranking. Da, Liu & Schaumburg (NY Fed)
  • Momentum has real crash risk. Momentum portfolios suffer infrequent, sharp, partly-forecastable crashes concentrated in market-rebound periods after drawdowns; the literature's fix is dynamic volatility-scaled exposure. Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016
  • A simple 200-day SMA gate genuinely works. Faber's mechanical 10-month/200-day trend filter cut maximum drawdown roughly in half versus buy-and-hold across asset classes, at some cost to raw CAGR — direct support for a SMA200-based hard filter. Faber, 2007

Where scorer.py stands

Momentum = SMA200/50/20 sign checks + RSI(14) zone + (month return > 0) + (quarter return > 0)

Three mismatches with the research above. First, there's no comparison to the S&P 500 or to sector — every input is self-referential, so the score can't distinguish a genuine momentum leader from a stock that's merely not falling. That's also a dropped requirement: the original Dec 18 spec explicitly called for "relative strength vs. SOXX," and it never made it into the Finviz rebuild. Second, the "month return > 0" test sits inside the exact one-month window the literature says to skip because of short-term reversal — it's rewarding a signal that's, if anything, mildly contrarian in the academic record. Third, 52-week-high proximity — arguably the single best-evidenced anchor above — isn't used at all, despite Finviz's own snapshot page already returning 52W High / 52W Low alongside the fields the scraper already pulls.

The falling-knife SMA200 filter, on the other hand, is squarely in line with Faber's finding and is worth keeping as-is.

2 · GARP & valuation

Real track record, softer academic footing, and a well-documented flaw in the core metric.

  • Lynch's record is the strongest single data point, but there's no Fama-French-style rigorous multi-decade factor backtest of "GARP" the way there is for value or momentum individually — it's a blend, and blends are inherently harder to isolate in the data than a single clean factor.
  • GARP's edge is highly time-period dependent. A CFA Institute retrospective found GARP behaved like a growth strategy from 1989–2001 and "more like value" after the dot-com bust — and that a meaningful share of its apparent edge simply comes from screening out negative-earnings stocks, not from the PEG calculation itself. CFA Institute, 2019
  • Growth + low P/E alone isn't a distinctive edge. Portfolios formed at the simple intersection of high earnings growth and low P/E don't show outperformance on their own in direct academic tests — the "reasonable price" half needs to be paired with a quality signal to show a robust edge, which is a direct segue into the gap below.
  • PEG = 1.0 is a rule of thumb, not a derived threshold. It shows up repeatedly in the literature described exactly that way — a convention, not an empirically fit cutoff.
  • The growth half of PEG is a forward analyst estimate — and those are systematically optimistic. Analyst forecast optimism is well documented and gets worse the further out and the higher-growth the estimate; there's also a measurable trade-off where more optimistic forecasts are, on average, less accurate. Hong & Kubik

Where scorer.py stands

Valuation = Forward P/E (≤15 → 15pt · ≤25 → 10pt · ≤40 → 5pt) + PEG (≤1.2 → 20pt · ≤1.8 → 12pt · ≤2.5 → 5pt)

The bands themselves are reasonable, conventional GARP thresholds. The real exposure is that a PEG of, say, 1.1 gets the full 20 points with no cross-check against whether the embedded growth estimate is even plausible — comparing forward growth to trailing growth, or fading unusually high estimates, would directly address the optimism-bias finding above rather than trusting the consensus number at face value.

3 · Balance sheet & quality

The single biggest blind spot in the current model.

  • Profitability is its own return-predictive factor — comparable in strength to value — and a combined quality+value strategy roughly doubles the monthly excess return of either alone (0.61%/mo combined vs. 0.28%/0.31% solo, in Novy-Marx's original sample). Novy-Marx, 2013
  • Piotroski's F-score — nine binary profitability/leverage/efficiency checks — added ~7.5%/yr to value strategies in the original study and ~10%/yr in more recent international out-of-sample tests, nearly two decades later. Piotroski F-Score, international evidence
  • "Growth bought with debt" has a precise academic answer, and it isn't a debt ratio. Sloan's accrual anomaly shows firms whose earnings are backed by real cash flow (low accruals) outperform firms whose earnings growth leans more on accounting accruals by roughly 10–12%/yr — a more targeted test of earnings quality than solvency ratios alone. Sloan, 1996; Dechow, Khimich & Sloan review
  • Interest coverage identifies a distinct distress category debt/equity misses. "Zombie firm" research classifies companies with interest coverage below 1x as structurally distressed regardless of their leverage ratio — a company can carry a fine debt/equity ratio and still not earn enough to cover its interest expense. Altman Z-score & interest coverage
  • The closest commercial analogue to this app bakes quality in explicitly. The iShares MSCI USA Quality GARP ETF markets its profitability/quality screen — alongside growth and value — as the actual differentiator versus a plain growth index. iShares GARP methodology note

Where scorer.py stands

Balance sheet = Debt/Equity (≤0.5 → 10pt · ≤1.0 → 7pt · ≤2.0 → 3pt) + Current Ratio (≥1.0 → 5pt)

This bucket is a solvency check, not a quality check — it asks "is this company over-levered?" but never "is this company's growth actually good?" No ROE, ROIC, or margin signal; no interest coverage; no earnings-quality proxy. That last one is also a dropped requirement — the original spec explicitly wanted interest coverage and a dilution check, and neither survived the Finviz rebuild. The frustrating part is the fix is mostly free: Finviz's snapshot page already returns ROA, ROE, ROIC, Gross Margin, Oper. Margin, and Profit Margin — the scraper pulls the full table already and simply never reads these keys back out.

4 · Combining signals

The one part of the architecture that's already correct.

  • Value and momentum are negatively correlated but independently profitable. Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen found roughly −0.17 correlation between the two in the US (similarly negative globally) — combining them smooths performance rather than just averaging the returns. Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013
  • An integrated single score beats separately-screened sub-portfolios. AQR's "don't just mix, integrate" research found that one blended signal — resolving factor trade-offs together at the stock level — outperforms combining three separately-selected screens. This directly validates building one FinalScore rather than running independent Value/Momentum/Quality screens and intersecting the results.
Combining factors reduces the risk of prolonged underperformance because they're imperfectly correlated — when value underperforms, momentum often outperforms, and vice versa.— AQR, on multi-factor integration

Nothing to fix here structurally. The gap upstream (§3) is which factors feed the blend, not the blending mechanism itself — adding a Quality bucket is a fourth input into the same architecture, not a redesign of it.

5 · Risk management

Screening candidates and managing a held position are different problems — the model only does the first.

  • Stop-loss efficacy is genuinely mixed in the data — stop-losses measurably reduce the disposition-effect tendency to hold losers too long, but don't unambiguously improve risk-adjusted returns, and can cut off recoveries. Worth having for discipline, not because the literature promises added return. Disposition effect & stop-losses
  • ~20–30 holdings diversifies away most idiosyncratic risk for a stock-picking portfolio — worth keeping in mind when actually sizing "fixed capital rotated between positions" so weekly rotation doesn't quietly concentrate into a handful of names. Statman, diversification literature
  • Published anomalies decay after publication — an average ~50% return decline post-publication across 97 tested predictors, with larger decay for the strongest in-sample performers. PEG, momentum, and RSI have all been public for 20–40 years; a reasonable expectation-setter, not a reason to abandon the approach. McLean & Pontiff, 2016

Where the app stands

The falling-knife filter and LateLegPenalty are reasonable, proportionate nods to crash/reversal risk on the selection side. But nothing in scorer.py tracks a position once you're in it — no entry price, no unrealized gain, no exit signal. The original Dec 18 spec explicitly called for "defined profit-taking + loss-cutting rules," and that requirement never made it past the strategy-definition stage into code. The app today only answers "what should I buy," never "should I still be holding this."

6 · Sector comparability & picking within a sector

Added after the Sectors-tab rollup raised a sharper question: is a fixed valuation band even valid across sectors, and does a strong sector mean "just buy the ETF"?

Is P/E-style valuation even comparable across sectors?

Not uniformly. Industry-specific valuation research finds P/E is the preferred multiple in roughly 20 of 25 industry groups — but the exceptions are exactly the sectors currently sitting at the top of the rollup. Banks and insurers are conventionally valued on Price/Book, not P/E, because leverage is the business model, not a risk to price around. REITs are valued on P/FFO or P/AFFO, because real-estate depreciation is a large non-cash charge that distorts GAAP net income into near-meaninglessness. Energy, materials, and telecom are conventionally valued on EV/EBITDA rather than P/E — and even that needs mid-cycle normalization, since trailing or forward earnings at a commodity-cycle extreme can make the multiple look artificially cheap or expensive relative to a normalized run rate. Sector applicability of EV/EBITDA and other multiples · REIT FFO/AFFO valuation

What this looks like in the live data at the time of writing

A direct check of the current run: 52% of Energy stocks (11 of 21) and 42% of Financial Services stocks (29 of 69) are tied at the maximum 25/25 Valuation score — versus 22% in Technology. The underlying PEG ratios inside Energy alone span 0.27 to 3.94, a real spread the fixed bands can no longer see once a stock clears the top threshold. That compression is a plausible reason Energy and Financial Services currently lead the sector rollup: it isn't only that they're strong, it's that the scoring runs out of room to tell "cheap" from "cheapest" in exactly the two sectors where P/E-style valuation is on the shakiest methodological ground to begin with.

Does a hot sector mean you should just buy the ETF?

The evidence points at both layers mattering, not one instead of the other:

  • Sector strength is real, and often larger than stock-specific momentum. Restating §1: once industry effects are controlled for, individual-stock momentum profits become "significantly weaker, and for the most part, statistically insignificant" (Moskowitz & Grinblatt, 1999).
  • But stripping sector/style exposure out and ranking on what's left — "residual momentum" — beats total-return momentum; it doesn't lose to it. Blitz, Huij & Martens found residual momentum earns risk-adjusted profits roughly twice as large as total-return momentum, is more consistent over time, and is less concentrated in the crash-prone extremes — a result that has held up out-of-sample globally since publication. Blitz, Huij & Martens, 2011
  • Long-standing practitioner precedent agrees. William O'Neil's CANSLIM system — in continuous use since the 1980s — makes this an explicit rule: buy the leading stocks in leading industry groups, not the sector average. Practitioner heuristic, not peer-reviewed, but directly on point. CANSLIM overview

Put together: a hot sector is a reason to be looking there, not a reason to stop at the ETF. The research specifically rewards finding the strongest names within the strongest sectors — which the app doesn't yet surface directly (gaps #10–11 below).

The standard fix: sector-relative z-scoring

Subtract the sector mean from each stock's raw metric and divide by the sector's standard deviation, so a stock is ranked against its own peers rather than the whole market. It directly addresses the compression problem above. The pitfall to know going in: it doesn't guarantee equal representation across sectors in a single combined ranking, and it isn't worth applying to metrics that don't actually vary much by sector to begin with. Recommended next steps

Ordered for maximum evidence-backed improvement per unit of effort.

  1. Stop defaulting missing data to "neutral" — surface it instead.Flag fallback-derived scores in the data model and show it in the dashboard (e.g. a muted "est." marker) rather than rendering it identically to a real measurement. Directly closes gap #2.
  2. Add a Quality/Profitability bucket from data already being scraped.ROE, ROIC, and margin fields are sitting unused in the Finviz response today. Closes gap #1, the highest-leverage change in this review.
  3. Add 52-week-high proximity to Momentum.Also already available from Finviz (52W High/52W Low). Closes gap #6.
  4. Add relative strength vs. SPY and sector.Requires fetching one extra snapshot (SPY) and comparing perf fields; restores the original spec's "vs. SOXX" requirement in more general form. Closes gap #3.
  5. Scope position tracking as its own feature.Entry price, unrealized gain/loss, and exit signals need a "what I hold" data model the screener doesn't have today — worth a separate design pass rather than folding into scorer.py. Closes gap #4.
  6. Add a within-sector rank/percentile badge.Pure client-side computation from data already loaded — no scoring change, no backend change. Closes gap #10.
  7. Sector-relative z-score Valuation and Quality as a second lens alongside the absolute score.Same PEG/Fwd P/E/ROIC/ROE/Margin inputs, normalized within sector instead of (or in addition to) fixed global bands — directly fixes the ceiling-compression shown above. Closes gap #11.
